Beyond Treasuries, the two other major bond families are corporate and municipal bonds.

**Corporate bonds** are issued by companies to fund operations and expansion. Because companies can fail, corporates pay higher yields than Treasuries — the extra yield is the **credit spread**.

- **Investment grade (BBB- and above):** Issued by financially solid firms; modest extra yield with low default rates.
- **High yield / "junk" (BB+ and below):** Issued by riskier firms; significantly higher yields but real default risk, and prices tend to fall alongside stocks in recessions — reducing their diversification value.

**Municipal bonds ("munis")** are issued by states, cities, and local agencies to fund schools, roads, and utilities. Their headline feature is tax treatment: interest is **exempt from federal income tax**, and usually from state tax if you live in the issuing state.

- Munis make the most sense for investors in high tax brackets holding bonds in taxable accounts.
- **Tax-equivalent yield** comparison: a 3% muni equals a 4.6% taxable bond for someone in the 35% bracket (3% ÷ (1 − 0.35)).
- Default rates on investment-grade munis are historically very low.

**Practical guidance:** Individual corporate and muni bonds trade with wide spreads and require credit analysis, so most individuals are better served by diversified bond funds or ETFs. Never reach for yield without understanding the added risk — yield is the price of risk, not free income.
