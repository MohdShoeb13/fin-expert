title: How Marginal Tax Brackets Actually Work
category: tax

The most widespread tax myth: "a raise can push me into a higher bracket and leave me with less money." It cannot — because U.S. brackets are **marginal**.

**How marginal really works:** Each rate applies only to the income *within* that bracket's range. With (illustrative 2025) single-filer brackets of 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%…, someone with $50,000 of taxable income does **not** pay 22% on everything. They pay 10% on the first ~$11,900, 12% up to ~$48,500, and 22% only on the last ~$1,500. Their **effective rate** lands near 13% even though their **marginal rate** is 22%.

**Why the distinction matters for financial decisions:**

- **A raise is always worth taking** — only the new dollars are taxed at the higher rate.
- **Traditional 401(k)/IRA contributions** save tax at your *marginal* rate (the top slice), making them more valuable for high earners.
- **Roth vs. Traditional** is fundamentally a bet comparing today's marginal rate against your expected rate in retirement.
- **The standard deduction** (~$15,000 single / ~$30,000 married in 2025) comes off the top before brackets apply at all — many retirees with modest withdrawals owe surprisingly little.

**Capital gains stack separately:** long-term gains and qualified dividends use their own 0/15/20% brackets sitting "on top of" ordinary income — careful retirees can realize substantial gains in the 0% band.

Knowing your marginal rate is the key input for nearly every account-choice and timing decision in personal finance.
