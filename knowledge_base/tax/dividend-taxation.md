---
title: How Dividends Are Taxed
category: tax
source: Adapted from IRS Topic 404 and Investopedia
---

Dividends are taxable in the year received — even if automatically reinvested — but the rate depends on the dividend's classification.

**Qualified dividends** get the favorable long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20%). To qualify, the dividend must come from a U.S. corporation (or qualifying foreign company) and you must have held the stock for more than 60 days around the ex-dividend date. Most dividends from ordinary stocks and broad index funds are qualified.

**Ordinary (non-qualified) dividends** are taxed at your full income tax rate (up to 37%). Common sources:

- **REIT dividends** (mostly ordinary — a key reason REITs belong in IRAs).
- **Bond fund interest distributions** (technically interest, taxed as ordinary income).
- Dividends on shares held only briefly.
- Money market fund distributions.

**Practical implications:**

- Your broker's annual **1099-DIV** splits the two categories for you; tax software handles the rest.
- High-dividend strategies in taxable accounts create an unavoidable annual tax drag; growth-oriented index funds defer more of their return into untaxed appreciation.
- The 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax applies to dividends above ~$200k (single) / $250k (married) income.
- Municipal bond fund dividends are federally tax-exempt — the exception to the rules above.

**Asset-location takeaway:** income-heavy assets (REITs, bond funds, high-dividend funds) fit best inside tax-advantaged accounts, leaving tax-efficient index funds for the taxable side.
