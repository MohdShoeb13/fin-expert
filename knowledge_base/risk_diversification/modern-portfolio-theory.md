Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), introduced by Harry Markowitz in 1952 (Nobel Prize, 1990), formalized a deceptively deep idea: **judge investments by their effect on the whole portfolio, not in isolation.**

**Core concepts, translated:**

- **Expected return & volatility:** Every asset offers some average return with some degree of wiggle (measured as standard deviation). More expected return generally demands accepting more wiggle.
- **Correlation — the magic ingredient:** What matters is how assets move *together*. Two volatile assets that zig at different times can combine into a calm portfolio. This is the mathematical engine behind diversification.
- **The efficient frontier:** Among all possible portfolios, some are simply better — more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return. The curve of these "best possible" combinations is the efficient frontier; rational investors should want to be on it, choosing their spot by risk appetite.
- **Systematic vs. idiosyncratic risk:** Diversification erases company-specific (idiosyncratic) risk for free, but market-wide (systematic) risk always remains — that's the risk investors are actually paid to bear.

**Honest limitations:** MPT relies on estimates of future returns and correlations that are unstable — correlations notoriously spike toward 1 in crises, exactly when diversification is most needed; and real return distributions have fatter tails (more extreme events) than the theory's neat bell curves assume.

**What survives the criticism — the practical takeaways:** diversify broadly, mix imperfectly correlated asset classes (stocks + bonds), think in portfolios rather than positions, and accept that only systematic risk is reliably rewarded. Index-fund investing is, in effect, MPT made cheap and automatic.
