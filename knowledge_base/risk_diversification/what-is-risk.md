In everyday speech "risk" means danger. In investing it has a more precise and more useful meaning: **uncertainty of outcomes** — and it comes in several distinct flavors that call for different defenses.

**Market risk (volatility):** The whole market falls — recessions, panics, rate shocks. Cannot be diversified away within stocks; managed through asset allocation (adding bonds/cash) and, above all, **time horizon**. Historically, U.S. stock returns have been positive in roughly 75% of years and in every 15-year period.

**Company-specific risk:** One company implodes (fraud, disruption, bankruptcy). Almost entirely eliminated by diversification — a 500-stock fund barely notices one failure that would devastate a concentrated holder.

**Inflation risk:** "Safe" cash quietly loses purchasing power. Counterintuitively, avoiding all market risk *guarantees* this one. The biggest risk for long horizons is being too conservative.

**Interest-rate risk:** Bond prices fall when rates rise (see duration).

**Liquidity risk:** Being unable to sell quickly at fair value (private assets, micro-caps, real estate).

**Behavioral risk — usually the biggest:** Panic-selling at the bottom, performance-chasing at the top. Studies (e.g., DALBAR, Morningstar's "Mind the Gap") consistently find real investors earn 1–2% less per year than the very funds they own, purely from poorly timed moves.

**The core insight:** risk cannot be eliminated, only chosen. The goal is taking *compensated* risks (broad market exposure) while diversifying away *uncompensated* ones (single stocks), in proportions you can hold through a crash without flinching.
