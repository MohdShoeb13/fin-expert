---
title: Assessing Your Risk Tolerance
category: risk_diversification
source: Adapted from FINRA and CFP Board guidance
---

Risk tolerance is your genuine capacity — financial *and* emotional — to endure portfolio losses without abandoning your plan. Misjudging it is one of the most expensive mistakes in investing, because the damage arrives at the worst moment: selling in a panic near a market bottom.

**Three distinct components:**

1. **Risk capacity (objective):** Can your finances absorb a loss? Determined by time horizon, income stability, emergency fund, and obligations. A 28-year-old with stable income has high capacity; a retiree drawing on the portfolio has low capacity.
2. **Risk attitude (emotional):** How do losses *feel*? The test isn't a questionnaire answered in a bull market — it's: "My $100,000 is now $65,000 after a year of relentless bad news. Do I truly stay the course?" Behavioral research shows losses hurt roughly twice as much as gains please (loss aversion), and most people overestimate their courage.
3. **Risk need:** How much risk do your goals actually require? Someone on track may not need to take more.

**Rule:** your allocation should be set by the *lowest* of the three.

**Practical calibration:** A useful heuristic — take your maximum tolerable loss, double it, and that's roughly your maximum stock percentage (stocks can fall ~50%). Comfortable losing at most 20%? Cap stocks near 40–50%.

**Signs you've overshot:** checking the portfolio compulsively during downturns, losing sleep, being tempted to "go to cash until things settle." The best portfolio is not the theoretically optimal one — it's the riskiest one you'll actually hold through a crisis.
