title: International Diversification
category: risk_diversification

U.S. investors often hold only U.S. stocks — a concentration known as **home-country bias**. International diversification spreads risk across the roughly 40% of global stock market value that sits outside the United States.

**The case for going global:**

- **Leadership rotates by decade.** International stocks beat U.S. stocks through the 1980s and 2000s; U.S. dominated the 1990s and 2010s. Nobody reliably predicts the next winner.
- **The "lost decade" lesson:** From 2000–2009 the S&P 500 returned roughly zero while emerging markets more than doubled. A global portfolio kept compounding.
- **Different economies, different cycles:** Currencies, interest rates, demographics, and sectors differ — imperfect correlation is exactly what diversification needs.

**The categories:**

- **Developed markets:** Europe, Japan, Australia, Canada — mature economies, behavior broadly similar to U.S. stocks.
- **Emerging markets:** China, India, Brazil, Taiwan — higher growth potential, higher volatility, additional political and currency risk.

**How much?** Common guidance ranges from 20% to 40% of the stock allocation in international funds (Vanguard suggests ~40%, matching global market weight; many investors settle around 30%). A single "total international" index fund covers both developed and emerging markets at ~0.05–0.10% cost.

**Honest counterpoints:** U.S. multinationals already earn ~40% of revenue abroad, and correlations have risen in the globalized era — international diversification helps most over decades, not months. Currency swings add short-term noise that roughly washes out long-term.
