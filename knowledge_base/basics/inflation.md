---
title: Inflation and Why Cash Loses Value
category: basics
source: Adapted from Federal Reserve education materials and Investopedia
---

Inflation is the gradual rise in prices over time, which means each dollar buys less in the future than it does today. The U.S. Federal Reserve targets about 2% annual inflation, but it has run much higher in some periods (over 9% in mid-2022).

**The math of erosion:** At 3% inflation, prices double roughly every 24 years. $100,000 held in cash today will have the purchasing power of about $74,000 in ten years and $55,000 in twenty.

**Why this matters for investors:**

- **Cash is not "safe" long-term.** A savings account paying 1% during 3% inflation loses 2% of purchasing power per year — a guaranteed slow loss.
- **Real return is what counts.** Real return = nominal return − inflation. A bond yielding 4% during 3% inflation has a real return of only 1%.
- **Stocks are a long-term inflation hedge.** Companies can raise prices, so corporate earnings — and stock values — tend to grow with or above inflation over long horizons.
- **Fixed payments suffer most.** Long-term bonds and fixed annuities lose real value when inflation surprises to the upside.

Specialized tools exist for inflation protection, such as TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities), whose principal adjusts with the Consumer Price Index, and I Savings Bonds. The key beginner takeaway: holding significant long-term savings entirely in cash is itself a risky position.
