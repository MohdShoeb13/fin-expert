---
title: Behavioral Biases Every Investor Should Know
category: basics
source: Adapted from behavioral finance research (Kahneman, Thaler) and FINRA education
---

The biggest threat to your returns is usually not the market — it's your own brain. Behavioral finance has cataloged the systematic mistakes humans make with money.

**Loss aversion:** Losses hurt about twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. This drives panic-selling at bottoms and explains why checking your portfolio daily makes you miserable (down days feel worse than up days feel good).

**Recency bias:** We project the recent past forward. After a crash, more crashing feels inevitable (so people sell low); after a boom, endless gains feel assured (so people buy high and over-risk).

**Overconfidence:** Most traders believe they're above average; the data says frequent traders underperform. Barber and Odean's classic study found the most active traders earned ~6% less per year than buy-and-hold investors.

**Herding:** Crowds feel safe — buying meme stocks at the peak, fleeing in 2009 — but markets reward the patient, not the popular.

**Confirmation bias:** We seek information agreeing with what we already believe, turning research into rationalization.

**Anchoring:** Fixating on a reference price ("I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid") that the market doesn't know or care about.

**Defenses that actually work:** automate contributions and rebalancing, write an investment policy statement *before* a crisis, check the portfolio rarely, and prefer boring diversified funds that leave nothing to decide in the heat of the moment. Good systems beat good intentions.
