---
title: "Market Capitalization: Large, Mid, and Small Caps"
category: stocks

Market capitalization ("market cap") measures a company's total stock market value: share price × number of shares outstanding. A company with 1 billion shares at $50 each has a $50 billion market cap.

**Size categories (approximate U.S. conventions):**

- **Mega cap ($200B+):** Apple, Microsoft — global giants.
- **Large cap ($10B–$200B):** Established leaders; the S&P 500 is mostly large caps.
- **Mid cap ($2B–$10B):** Established but still growing; often considered a sweet spot of growth and stability.
- **Small cap ($300M–$2B):** Younger or niche companies; higher growth potential, higher volatility, tracked by the Russell 2000.
- **Micro cap (under $300M):** Very risky, thinly traded; a frequent home of fraud and pump-and-dump schemes.

**Why it matters:**

- **Risk/return profile differs by size.** Small caps historically returned somewhat more than large caps over very long periods, with substantially larger swings.
- **Index weighting.** Most index funds are cap-weighted: the bigger the company, the bigger its share of the fund. Apple alone can be ~6–7% of an S&P 500 fund.
- **Diversification.** A "total market" fund holds all sizes at once, which is the simplest way to avoid choosing.

Note that share price alone says nothing about size: a $900 stock can belong to a smaller company than a $30 stock with far more shares outstanding.
