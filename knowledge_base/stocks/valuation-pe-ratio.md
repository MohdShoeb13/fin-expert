Valuation metrics help answer: "Is this stock expensive or cheap relative to the business behind it?"

**Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio** is the most cited metric: share price ÷ earnings per share. A P/E of 20 means you pay $20 for every $1 of annual profit. The S&P 500's long-run average P/E is roughly 15–20.

- **High P/E (30+):** The market expects strong future growth — common for tech companies. The risk is paying for growth that never arrives.
- **Low P/E (under 12):** The stock may be undervalued… or the market may correctly expect earnings to decline (a "value trap").

**Other common metrics:**

- **P/B (price-to-book):** Price vs. net assets; useful for banks and industrials.
- **P/S (price-to-sales):** Useful for companies without profits yet.
- **PEG ratio:** P/E ÷ earnings growth rate; a rough way to compare growth stocks (around 1.0 is considered fair).
- **EPS (earnings per share):** Company profit divided by share count — the "E" in P/E.

**Important caveats:** Metrics are only meaningful when compared within the same industry; software firms always trade at higher P/Es than utilities. No single ratio "works" reliably — professional investors use them as starting points for research, not buy signals. For beginners in index funds, valuation analysis is largely handled by simply owning the whole market.
