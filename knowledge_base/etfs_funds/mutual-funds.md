title: Mutual Funds Explained

A mutual fund pools money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio managed under a stated strategy. Unlike ETFs, mutual fund shares are bought and sold directly with the fund company once per day at the closing **NAV (net asset value)** — the per-share value of everything the fund owns.

**Two fundamentally different types:**

- **Index funds:** Passively track a benchmark like the S&P 500. Minimal trading, minimal cost (often 0.02–0.15% per year).
- **Actively managed funds:** A manager picks investments trying to beat the market, charging 0.5–1.5%+ per year for the effort.

**The evidence on active management is stark:** SPIVA scorecards consistently show that over 15-year periods, roughly 85–90% of actively managed U.S. stock funds underperform their benchmark index, largely because of fees. Past outperformance also shows little persistence — last decade's star manager is usually not the next one.

**Costs to watch:**

- **Expense ratio:** The annual fee, deducted invisibly from returns.
- **Load:** A sales commission (up to 5.75%) on some funds — avoid loaded funds entirely; excellent no-load alternatives always exist.
- **12b-1 fees:** Marketing fees baked into some expense ratios.

**Mutual fund vs. ETF:** Functionally similar for index investing. Mutual funds allow automatic dollar-amount investing (great for payroll contributions, which is why 401(k)s use them); ETFs offer intraday trading and better tax efficiency in taxable accounts.
