---
title: What Is an ETF?
category: etfs_funds
source: Adapted from SEC investor.gov and Investopedia
---

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a basket of securities — stocks, bonds, or other assets — that trades on a stock exchange like a single stock. Buying one share of an S&P 500 ETF instantly gives you a sliver of 500 companies.

**Why ETFs became the default building block for modern portfolios:**

- **Instant diversification:** One purchase can cover an entire market.
- **Low cost:** Major index ETFs charge expense ratios of 0.03–0.10% per year ($3–$10 per $10,000 invested).
- **Liquidity:** Trade any time during market hours at a visible price.
- **Tax efficiency:** ETF structure minimizes taxable capital gains distributions (important in taxable accounts).
- **Transparency:** Holdings are published daily.
- **No minimums:** One share — or a fraction of one — is enough to start.

**Common ETF categories:** broad market (total U.S. stock, S&P 500), international, bond, sector (technology, healthcare), dividend-focused, and commodity ETFs.

**Cautions:** Leveraged and inverse ETFs (names with "2x," "3x," or "short") are day-trading instruments that decay over time and are unsuitable for buy-and-hold investors. Exotic, narrow thematic ETFs often carry high fees and arrive after the theme has already run up. For beginners, broad, boring, and cheap is the winning combination.
