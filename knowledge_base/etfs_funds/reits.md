A REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust) is a company that owns and operates income-producing real estate — apartment complexes, office towers, warehouses, data centers, cell towers, shopping centers — and trades on stock exchanges like any other share.

**The defining rule:** To qualify as a REIT, a company must distribute at least **90% of its taxable income to shareholders as dividends**. This makes REITs natural income investments, typically yielding 3–5%, well above the broad stock market.

**Why investors hold REITs:**

- **Real estate exposure with liquidity:** Sell in seconds, no tenants, no maintenance calls, $100 minimum instead of $100,000 down payments.
- **Income:** High, relatively steady dividends.
- **Diversification:** Real estate returns don't move in lockstep with stocks or bonds, though REITs still fall in broad market crashes.
- **Inflation tendency:** Rents and property values often rise with inflation over time.

**Sectors matter:** Data-center and warehouse REITs boomed in the 2010s–2020s while office REITs were hurt by remote work. A broad REIT index fund diversifies across sectors.

**Tax note:** REIT dividends are mostly taxed as ordinary income (not at lower qualified-dividend rates), so REITs are best held inside tax-advantaged accounts like IRAs.

Total-market index funds already include REITs at ~3–4% weight; a dedicated REIT fund is an optional tilt, commonly capped at 5–10% of a portfolio.
